It is important to note that there are two kinds of problems: impersonal and personal. The impersonal deals with the satisfaction of conceptual constraints that may exist independent of any form of value judgement, one example being a math problem. This organisation however deals only with the latter kind of problem: the personal kind defined purely within subjectivity, that problem which ultimately underlies all states of unhappiness. It manifests as certain kind of mental phenomena predicated with a sense of identity. To better understand this kind of problem I will introduce a model of existence

> ## Problems
> There are at least two different definitions for *problem*: the first is of an impersonal kind. For instance, a an unsolved algebraic equation or a puzzle. The phenomena is considered a *problem to be solved*, regardless of whether or not it is perceived by an individual. This is not the kind of problem being referred to.
> 
> The second and more pertinent definition is of the kind which arises as a subjective experience itself. The definition is as follows:
> 
> > **Problem**
> > 1. a matter or situation regarded as unwelcome or harmful and needing to be dealt with and overcome. (Oxford Languages, via Google)
> 
> A slight modification of this definition is necessary. Here, the object of experience is itself regarded as the problem, but it will be inverted: a problem is regarded as the *subjective experience of regarding* a thing or situation as problematic itself.
> 
> This experience of regarding may be gross or subtle, conscious or subconscious. When a person is suffering, they are aware of the problematic nature of that experience by way of their own judgement. But to a person experiencing delirium, that recognition is less prominent. Nonetheless, there is a mental tendency to "color" the experience as problematic, however subtle it may be. This quasi-volitional response to and creation of displeasurable phenomena is the kind of *problem* being referred to.
> 
> ## The Mechanics
> ```mermaid
> graph LR
>     subgraph Stress
>         Fabrication --Conditions--> Craving
>         Craving --Creates--> Displeasure
>         Displeasure --Triggers--> Craving
>     end

> 
> Negative, undesirable mental displeasure arising due to mental formations rooted in craving (attraction or aversion), and the mind reacting to that displeasure along with other displeasure over time defined as suffering of varying degrees. Even relishing in pleasure with joy is subtly suffering, as there is a subtle and continuous grasping at pleasure, which creates displeasure, and the mind reacts to that displeasure with aversion, but it is below a threshold so much that it is considered negligible. No obvious thought of objection occurs, one is quite thoroughly happy and satisfied. But it is objectively true that if there was a total stilling of craving-rooted formations, that would be of the highest quality of happiness as there is no objection at all, for all objection is rooted in craving. So if there is no craving, how could there be any objection, and thus any unhappiness at all?
> 
> The craving reactivity of the mind is the fundamental problem. It manifests as mental displeasure i.e. negative emotional states. When it is attenuated below a threshold it is happiness, which is often accompanied by external and/or internal pleasure, though with a slow burn of craving.

> # .
> 
> \"What do I ought to do?\" is only relevant to one for whom it matters. For it to matter to him he must be afflicted with a desire, passion, preference, inclination, longing, or tendency, of which comprises attraction to the pleasant and aversion to the unpleasant. The taking up of the unpleasant leads to the distress of negative emotions rooted in aversion. The taking up of the pleasant leads to the joy of positive emotions rooted in attraction. All which one of such ever does is the attraction to the pleasant and aversion to the unpleasant. Even in cases of seeming attraction to the unpleasant, it is in reality the attraction to a superimposed pleasure.
> 
> \"What do I ought to do?\" is a pleasant feeling which one clings to, perhaps in escape of the displeasure of \"I may do what I ought not to.\" Nonetheless, what one ought to do is a vocalisation of one\'s preference, the satisfaction of which is the only rational ought. But however a person may satisfy, he may still experience the visissitudes of life. And so thinking one would satisfy his desires in the present, he suffers displeasure in the future. An intelligent one ought not to do that which leads to his long term affliction, for it is intrinsically counterproductive. Acting in his long term wellfare is what one ought to do.
> 
> Now, we see four components to this wellfare: craving, perception, feeling, and uptake. A desirous person would perceive a pleasure and take it up for his enjoyment fulfilling his desire. But he may also perceive a displeasure but not take it up by subduing his craving. The latter in its complete form is the superior of the two, the more sastisfying of the two.
> 
> For the overcoming of unwholesome tendencies, one must perceive the drawbacks of what is normally seen as pleasant, upon which it becomes unpleasant by its superimposition of drawbacks. Failing to see the drawbacks would lead to his failure so long as he is still besot with craving.
> 
> The highest liberation would be gained by superimposing the drawbacks of all alternatievs and thus relinquishing them out of aversion, and by taking up the superimposed pleasure of the benefits of doing so, the benefits of that kind of attainment, and upon the atttainment of it the intrinsic drive itself would cease; one abandons the raft he made to cross over to the further shore, gaining freedom from the even raft itself let alone the river.
