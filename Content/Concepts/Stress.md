> ## Abstract
> There exist problematic emotions characterized by stress. That is, the stress of dissatisfaction: the inclination of the mind rooted in craving, accompanied by unpleasant feelings. From the most abject of suffering, all the way down to the subtlest of boredom, all such emotions are characterized by some level of stressful inclination, however subtle it may be. In fact, even emotions which are conventionally considered to be free of affliction, such as peace, happiness, and security, are in actuality still afflicted to a very subtle degree, so much so that it can be considered negligible. These are fine as long as they last, but with the utter destruction of all states of rooted inclination of the mind comes the traceless freedom of emancipation resultant of extinguishment. 
> 
> Unease is volitional. With all subjectivity being in relation to identity, be it gross or subtle, the subjective experience of unease ceases when there is no longer induvidual volitional activity.
> 
> Suffering is the festering of craving–volitional pressure. And in its absence, absolute ease.
> 
> This quality of stress can be measured by a single scalar value with zero as its lower bound, akin to the heat released by the process of combustion. 
> 
> Two kinds of feeling can be observed, with positive and negative emotions arising in relation to these: pleasant, with concomiant ease, and unpleasant, with concomiant unease. It should be noted that feeling is not synnonymous with sensation, for its perception of pleasure is dependent on different conditions which determine its quality. For example, the feeling of heat can be percieved as pleaseant or unpleasant depending on the circumstances.
> 
> Five kinds of sensation can be observed: sight, sound, smell, taste, and bodily sensations, with mental sensations as a sixth. It is in relation to these that there is pleasure and displeasure. Thus, the interaction between the senses and their objects, along with the perceptive faculty which transform them into feeling, and finally with the latent craving in the mind, for pleasure and against displeasure, form the causal mechanism by which stressful emotions arise.
> 
> By altering and controlling any of these three factors, one can minimize the occurance of stress. By controlling the objects of the senses to be those seen as pleasurable according to the circumstances, one can remain satisfied. By controlling the perceptive faculties, the generation of pleasure can become more flexible and robust. And with the purification of the mind, the attenuation of the root cause of the issue.
> 
> Insofar as these are governed by causality, and volition holds the capacity to affect this system, with knowledge as a guiding factor, one can optimize their sequence of volitional actions such that there is a minimization of all states of distress indefinitely through the future. And, with the potential attainment of an unconditioned state beyond the limitations of causality, permanantly unbound freedom beyond reckoning.
>

> ## Two Points of Leverage
> With the preference for pleasure and against pain remaining stable, we can then see all stress is a scalar combination of desire and feeling. One not used to fasting is beset with strong aversion to the bodily feeling of hunger, and strong attraction to the idea of eating and satiating their apitite. But for one well aquinted with this practice, that feeling of hunger is not felt nearly as displeasurable, and for some, even slightly pleasurable.
> 
> In another example, the winner of a competition is flooded by the pleasure of the idea of victory, they are elated and their desires are fulfilled. Here we see that the desires of a person are quelled by the influx of pleasure as the mind holds on lightly to a stable stream of feeling.
> 
> Thus, stress can be minimized by reducing desire and attenuating it with pleasure.

> The goal is to minimize distress. Distress is the acquisition of displeasure. Acquisition is reflexively distressing due to it being displeasurable. Thus, the only complete escape from distress is in the absence of acquisition.
>

> ### The Subtle Truth
> 
> It is easy and common to equate these two kinds of experiences directly to the objects they correspond to, but in reality, the sensations themselves are not intrinsic to them. What I observe is that choices rooted in aversion and attraction result in some level of mental stress which itself is subejct to aversion. Thus, it is by volition that there is a proliferation of diagreeable feelings -- reflexive agitation. When abiding in the agreeable, this volition is reduced and abated, while it flare up in the presense of the disagreeable. But with careful and ardent attention one is able to reduce and minimize these volitions which in turn decrease one\'s vexation regardless of the circumstances. This observation lines up with the teachings of the Buddha, and he declares that he found a way to permanantly end these volitions, thus putting an end to all suffering for good.
> 
> So we have two components to this phenomenon of reflexive agitation: volition rooted in attraction and aversion , and the sensations of the five kinds plus mind. By purifying volition and curating agreeable sensations, one can minimize this suffering by way of those conditions. But to remove the conditions for volition entirely, suffering can be permanantly brought to an end.
> 
> Mentally generated unpleasant feeling combined with an active volition withstanding that feeling. Yearning for pleasant feeling, giving rise to unpleasant feeling, withstanding that feeling, grasping after the other.
> 
> Ordinary happiness: pleasant feeling with a suspense of effort, subtle grasping of the feeling. Not enough to explicity cognize. Ultimate happiness: the total cessation of all effort, the feeling no longer matters. The suspense of effort is the core component of happiness. Thus, nibbana is the ultimate happiness.

> ## What is Stress?
> - Appropriated displeasure. That is, displeasure arising with a perception of "I-ness". These are the fundamental components of affliction.
> - The lesser the affliction, the better the state.
> - Appropriated pleasure is not stressful, but its side effects are, as desire itself can only arise in the unresolved form, and that form is unpleasant.
> - Stress is equivalent to dukkhadukkhata, but the latter forms of viparinama and sankhara are not. This definition only accounts for the phenomenal aspect, not the ontological.
